Three factors that drive customer choice over having the best

RH Written by Richard Haddad

No matter what industry your business is in, there's one thing we all have in common: our need for customers. It makes no difference how good your product is — without customers, the business doesn't survive.

Every company needs customers, and we are all competing over the same groups of people. What I find common in all of it is this mantra: "We are the best in…"

Gillette: The best a man can get. Mercedes-Benz: The best or nothing. BMW: The ultimate driving machine.

There's nothing really wrong with that, but my question is always: well, best for whom? Find your favourite restaurant on Google and read the reviews. The same burger gets two opposite verdicts from two different people. Trying to make your product "the best" may not always be the best way to find good customers.

1. Convenience: make life easier for your customers

Drive-thrus can account for up to 70% of a fast food restaurant's sales. The product may not be the best around, or even the cheapest, but the process of getting it is easy, so people come.

More and more people are opting for online shopping over visiting a bricks-and-mortar store. Libraries in Australia found digital lending grew by 48% over the past handful of years. Convenience isn't everything, but it is a very tempting offer against a competitor who doesn't offer it.

2. Practicality: make your product more usable

Remember the pen with four colours in it? Magical, at least to a primary school kid. After some use, the levers jammed, and holding the thing wasn't practical because of how big it was. I was happy to carry multiple pens, each of which wrote in one single colour. It was way more practical.

You often opt for practicality over a seemingly cheaper product or the "ultimate" problem solver. Ask your grandparents how much they love new smartphones over their old Nokia.

3. Trust: make your name familiar and reliable

If I could stress any factor of your business, it would be this. Be a business your customers can always count on.

A customer once called and said they had looked for a web developer closer to them, but months later the work still hadn't been done. They decided to bring their business back to me, because "he gets the job done".

Unlike the other two factors, trust is in fact everything. It doesn't matter how practical the purchase process is or how practical your product is. Break your customers' trust and, often, they will never return.

Conclusion

Every competitor will try to beat you on product and service. They'll drop prices, add features at the same price, throw in the bells and whistles. In the end, what customers look for is:

  • The convenience of the process
  • The practicality of your product
  • Most importantly of all, the trust you've built
