## Article 1: When Should You Redesign Your Business Website? 7 Warning Signs

Let me paint a picture for you.

You built your website a few years ago. Maybe five. Maybe eight. At the time, it was great. You were proud of it. You shared the link with everyone. You probably even refreshed your analytics a dozen times that first week just to watch the numbers tick up.

But somewhere along the way, you stopped looking at it. You stopped sharing it. And now, when someone asks for your website, you find yourself saying something like, "Yeah, it's a bit outdated, but..."

That "but" is the warning sign.

I've been building websites for over 25 years now, and I've had hundreds of conversations with business owners who knew, deep down, their site needed work. They just weren't sure if they needed a fresh coat of paint or a full demolition. So let me walk you through the signs that tell you it's time for a proper rebuild, not just a tweak.

### 1. Your site takes more than three seconds to load

Here's a number that should make you uncomfortable: 53% of mobile visitors will leave your site if it takes longer than three seconds to load. Three seconds. That's barely enough time to take a sip of coffee.

And it's not just about impatient visitors. Google uses page speed as a ranking factor. So a slow site doesn't just lose you customers who arrive — it stops new ones from finding you in the first place.

If your site was built five or more years ago, there's a good chance it's carrying bloated code, oversized images, and outdated scripts that are dragging it down like an anchor. A refresh won't fix that. You need a rebuild with performance baked in from the ground up.

### 2. It looks terrible on a phone

Pull out your phone right now and load your website. Go on, I'll wait.

How does it look? Can you read the text without pinching and zooming? Do the buttons work with your thumb? Does the menu actually function?

If you're squinting, scrolling sideways, or tapping things three times to get them to work, you've got a problem. More than 60% of web traffic in Australia comes from mobile devices. If your site wasn't built mobile-first — and I mean genuinely mobile-first, not just "it sort of shrinks down" — you're turning away the majority of your potential customers.

### 3. Your enquiry rate has dropped off a cliff

This is the one that hurts the most, because you can feel it in your revenue.

A client of mine came to me last year saying their website enquiries had dried up. They were getting maybe one or two a month, down from ten or fifteen. They assumed it was a marketing problem. It wasn't. Their contact form had been broken for three months and nobody noticed, because nobody was testing it.

But even when forms work, declining enquiries often point to a deeper issue: your site no longer speaks to your audience. Their needs have changed, your competitors have upgraded, and your site is still sitting there talking about things nobody cares about anymore. That's not a tweak. That's a rethink.

### 4. You're embarrassed to share the URL

This one is simple, and you already know the answer. When you hand someone a business card, or when a potential client asks for your website, do you feel a little knot in your stomach?

Your website is your digital shopfront. Think of it like a physical office. If your office had peeling paint, a broken front door, and flickering lights, you'd fix it, wouldn't you? Because you know that first impression matters. Your website is no different. It's just that the peeling paint is a stock photo from 2016 and the broken door is a navigation menu that makes no sense.

### 5. You're getting security warnings

If visitors are seeing "Not Secure" in their browser bar, or worse, a full-page warning telling them your site might be dangerous — you're not just losing trust, you're actively scaring people away.

Expired SSL certificates, outdated CMS versions, vulnerable plugins — these aren't cosmetic problems. They're structural. Think of it like finding cracks in a load-bearing wall. As a structural engineer by training, believe me when I say: you don't patch structural problems. You address them properly.

### 6. You can't update your own content

Here's a test. Can you log into your website right now and update your phone number? Change a photo? Add a new service?

If the answer is "I'd have to email my developer" or "I don't even have the login anymore," your site is holding you hostage. A modern website should give you control over the basics. You shouldn't need a computer science degree to change your opening hours.

I've had business owners come to me who hadn't updated their site in years — not because they didn't want to, but because they literally couldn't. Their previous developer had built something so custom and so undocumented that nobody else could touch it. That's not clever development. That's a trap.

### 7. Your brand has evolved, but your site hasn't

Businesses grow. They change direction. They refine their offering, redefine their audience, maybe even rebrand entirely. But the website? It's still sitting there with the old logo, the old messaging, and the old colour scheme.

I see this constantly with established businesses. They've spent real money on new branding — new logo, new signage, new vehicle wraps, new uniforms — and then the website still looks like it belongs to a completely different company. It creates this jarring disconnect that makes potential customers feel like something's off, even if they can't articulate what.

### So when do you actually pull the trigger?

If you recognised yourself in one of these signs, keep an eye on things. If you nodded along to three or more? It's time.

And I want to be clear: a redesign doesn't mean starting from scratch emotionally. You're not throwing away everything you've built. You're taking everything you've learned about your business and your customers over the past few years and building something that actually reflects where you are now, not where you were then.

The businesses I work with — established Australian companies turning over $500K and up — they don't redesign because it's fun. They redesign because the cost of not redesigning is higher. Every month with a broken, slow, embarrassing site is a month of lost enquiries, lost trust, and lost revenue.

## Article 2: How to Write a Website Brief That Actually Gets You What You Want

I'm going to let you in on a secret that most web developers won't tell you.

The number one reason website projects go sideways — over budget, over time, and underwhelming — isn't bad design. It isn't dodgy code. It's a bad brief. Or more often, no brief at all.

I've been on the receiving end of hundreds of website briefs over 25 years. Some of them were brilliant. Clear, focused, gave me everything I needed to deliver something great. And some of them were... well, one of them was literally just "make it pop." That was the entire brief. Three words.

I made it pop. The client hated it. Because "pop" meant something completely different in their head than it did in mine.

So if you're about to invest in a new website — and if you're talking to someone reputable, you're probably looking at $5,000 to $15,000 or more — it's worth spending an hour writing a brief that actually sets the project up for success.

Here's how to do that.

### Start with the why, not the what

The first thing most people write in a brief is "I want a new website." Which, respectfully, tells me nothing. I already knew that. You contacted a web developer.

What I actually need to know is: why do you want a new website? What's driving this decision right now?

Are you losing business to competitors with better online presence? Has your brand changed and the site no longer matches? Are you launching a new service line? Are you embarrassed to send people to your current site?

The "why" shapes everything. A site designed to generate leads looks completely different from a site designed to establish credibility. A site for a business launching a new product is structured differently from one that's consolidating an existing offering. Tell me the problem you're trying to solve, and I can design a solution. Tell me you "want a new website" and I'm guessing.

### Know who you're talking to

Not who you are. Who your customers are.

This trips up so many business owners. They write pages about their history, their values, their team, their certifications. And that stuff matters, don't get me wrong. But the first question I ask is always: who is visiting this site, and what do they need?

Give me a profile. Not a marketing persona with a cute name — just plain facts. Are they business owners or consumers? What's their age range? Are they local to Brisbane, Australia-wide, or international? What problem do they have that you solve? What are they comparing you against?

If you serve multiple audiences — say, residential and commercial — tell me that. It changes how we structure the site.

### Show me what you like (and what you hate)

"I'll know it when I see it" is not a design direction. But "I like how this site feels clean and professional, and I hate how that site is cluttered with animations" — that's gold.

Spend 20 minutes looking at websites. Not just in your industry — any industry. Find three to five sites you like and write a sentence about what you like about each one. Is it the colour palette? The layout? The photography style? The way information is organised?

And just as importantly, show me what you don't like. If you despise dark backgrounds or autoplay video, say so now. Not after I've built the homepage around a cinematic video hero section.

### Be honest about your content

Here's where most projects stall, and I mean completely grind to a halt.

I can design and build a stunning website in four to six weeks. But I can't do that if I'm sitting around waiting for your content. And by content, I mean: the actual words on the pages, your high-resolution logo, professional photos of your team and work, case studies, testimonials, and any documents you want available for download.

In your brief, be brutally honest about where your content stands. Do you have professional copywriting? Or are you hoping someone will write it? Do you have professional photography, or are we working with phone photos? Is your logo print-ready, or is it a low-res JPEG from 2012?

There's no wrong answer here. I can recommend copywriters and photographers. I can work with what you have. But I need to know upfront so I can plan the project properly and quote you accurately.

### Talk about money

I know this one is uncomfortable. Australians especially seem to treat budgets like state secrets. But here's the thing: telling me your budget doesn't mean I'll spend all of it. It means I can tell you what's realistic.

If your budget is $3,000, I'll tell you honestly what you can get for that (and it might be less than you're imagining). If your budget is $15,000, I can plan a much more comprehensive build with custom functionality, professional content, and proper SEO foundations.

A vague brief with no budget is like walking into a builder and saying "I want a house." How many bedrooms? What suburb? What finishes? The answer to all of those is: depends on the budget.

### Set a timeline, but be realistic

"We need it by next month" is a timeline. It's also, in most cases, unrealistic for a quality build. Rushed websites are sloppy websites. They have broken links, inconsistent design, half-written content, and missing features that get forgotten in the chaos.

A realistic timeline for a professional business website is six to ten weeks from kickoff to launch. That includes design, development, content integration, testing, and revisions. If you have an event, a rebrand launch, or a seasonal deadline driving the timeline, put that in the brief. We can work backwards from it.

But if there's no hard deadline? Say that too. It takes the pressure off and usually results in a better final product.

### What happens when the brief is vague

Let me tell you what happens in practice when someone sends a vague brief.

The developer fills in the gaps with assumptions. They assume your audience. They assume your goals. They assume your preferred style. They build what they think you want, based on their best guess.

Then you see the first draft and say, "That's not what I had in mind." And now we're into revision cycles. The timeline blows out. The budget creeps up. Everyone gets frustrated. And the finished product is a compromise that nobody loves.

All because of a 30-minute conversation that never happened upfront.

### The brief doesn't need to be fancy

I want to be clear: I'm not asking you to write a 20-page document. Some of the best briefs I've received have been a single page, maybe two. Dot points are fine. Informal language is fine. You're not writing a tender response — you're having a conversation on paper.

If you can answer these questions, you've got a solid brief:

- Why do you need a new website?
- Who is it for?
- What do you want visitors to do when they get there?
- What sites do you like, and why?
- What content do you have ready?
- What's your budget range?
- When do you need it live?
- Is there anything you definitely want or definitely don't want?

That's it. Eight questions. You could answer them over a coffee.

I had a client ring me once — a trades business in Brisbane, solid reputation, been around for 15 years. They said they hadn't touched their website in three years. "It still works," they told me. "People can find our number. That's all we need."

The customer screenshotted it and posted it in a local Facebook group asking if the business was legit. Fifteen years of reputation, questioned publicly, because of a $15 SSL certificate that had expired.

That's what ignoring your website costs you. Not in theory. In reality.

### The myth of "set and forget"

Let me address this head-on, because I hear it constantly: "The website's done, we don't need to spend any more on it."

I understand the logic. You paid good money to have it built. It's live. It's working. Why would you keep paying?

Here's why. A website is not a brochure you print once and hand out for five years. It's a living piece of software running on a server that's connected to the internet 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. It has a content management system that needs updates. It has plugins that need patches. It has a security certificate that expires. It runs on PHP or Node.js versions that get deprecated. The server it sits on needs maintenance.

Ignoring all of that is like buying a car and never changing the oil. It'll run fine for a while. Then it won't.

I'm going to lay out what neglect actually costs, in Australian dollars, because I think it's important to see this clearly.

Usually between $0 and $100 per year, depending on the type. Most hosting plans include it free.

The business owner had no clue because they never received an error notification — the form just silently failed. How do you calculate the cost of leads you never knew you lost?

**Slow page speeds** — Google has been very clear about this. A one-second delay in page load time can result in a 7% reduction in conversions. Here's what makes website neglect particularly nasty: the problems compound.

An outdated plugin creates a security vulnerability. That vulnerability gets exploited. Now your site is serving malware to visitors. Google detects the malware and delists your site from search results. Your organic traffic drops to zero. You don't notice for two weeks because you don't check your analytics. By the time you find out, your domain has been blacklisted, your email deliverability has tanked because your domain reputation is shot, and you're looking at weeks of recovery.

Maintenance isn't a cost. It's insurance. And like all insurance, it feels like a waste of money right up until the moment you need it.

The question isn't "can I afford website maintenance?" The question is "can I afford to find out what happens without it?"

## Article 4: Managed Hosting vs Shared Hosting: What Brisbane Business Owners Actually Need to Know

Let me start with an analogy, because this topic is one of those things that sounds more complicated than it is.

Think of web hosting like renting space for your business.

Shared hosting is like renting a desk in a co-working space. It's cheap. You show up, there's wifi, there's air conditioning, and it works fine — until the bloke three desks over starts downloading massive files and the whole internet slows to a crawl. Or someone props the door open and now security is everyone's problem. You have no control over who else is in the building, and their behaviour directly affects you.

Managed hosting is like renting your own office in a well-run building. You've got your own space, your own resources, and a building manager who handles the maintenance, the security system, and the air conditioning. If something breaks at 2am, they fix it. You just show up and run your business.

That's the fundamental difference. Now let me get into the specifics, because if you're running a legitimate business website — not a hobby blog, not a side project, an actual business that depends on its online presence — this stuff matters more than most people realise.

### Speed: the difference you can feel

On shared hosting, your website shares server resources — CPU, memory, bandwidth — with potentially hundreds of other websites. During off-peak times, this is usually fine. But during busy periods? Your site slows down because everyone else on that server is busy too.

I've seen business websites on shared hosting that load in two seconds at 6am and eight seconds at 11am. Same site, same content, wildly different performance — because the other sites on the server are chewing through resources during business hours. Which, naturally, is exactly when your customers are trying to reach you.

Managed hosting allocates dedicated resources to your site. Your CPU, your memory, your bandwidth. If the site next door is having a traffic spike, that's their problem, not yours. Consistent, predictable performance regardless of what time it is.

### Security: not just your problem

Here's something that genuinely worries me about shared hosting, and it should worry you too.

On a shared server, if one website gets hacked, it can compromise every other site on that server. It's called cross-site contamination, and it's more common than hosting companies like to admit. One vulnerable WordPress site with an outdated plugin can be the doorway that gives an attacker access to the entire server.

With managed hosting — good managed hosting — your site is isolated. Your environment is yours. Security updates are applied proactively. Firewalls are configured specifically for your setup. Malware scanning runs regularly. And if something does go wrong, there's a team that responds immediately, not a ticket system that gets back to you in 24 to 48 hours.

For a business in Brisbane handling customer data, enquiry forms, or any kind of transaction, this isn't optional. It's a duty of care.

### Uptime: what "99.9%" actually means

Every hosting company promises "99.9% uptime." It sounds impressive. It also means your site can be down for nearly nine hours a year and they're still meeting their promise.

The difference is what happens during that downtime. With shared hosting, you submit a ticket. You wait. Maybe it gets resolved in an hour, maybe in twelve. You have no visibility into what's happening and no control over the timeline.

With managed hosting, you typically get proactive monitoring — meaning the hosting team knows your site is down before you do. They're working on it before you've even noticed. And because your environment is isolated, the fix is usually faster because they're not trying to untangle a problem across 200 sites.

For context: if your business generates $50,000 a month through its website and you're down for six hours during a business day, that's roughly $1,500 in lost revenue. That figure should inform your hosting decision more than the monthly price difference.

### Support: the thing you don't think about until you need it

Shared hosting support is, generally speaking, generic. They can help you with server-level issues, but they don't know your website. They don't know your CMS, your plugins, your custom code. If your WordPress site throws an error, their response is usually "that's an application-level issue, contact your developer."

Managed hosting support — at least the kind worth paying for — is different. The support team knows your stack. They can troubleshoot CMS issues, help with SSL configurations, assist with email deliverability problems, and proactively advise on performance improvements. They're not just keeping a server running; they're keeping your website running.

### Australian servers: closer is faster

This one's straightforward but often overlooked.

If your customers are in Brisbane, Sydney, Melbourne — anywhere in Australia — your website should be hosted on servers in Australia. Every millisecond of latency between the server and the visitor adds up. A site hosted in the US or Europe will always be slower for Australian visitors than one hosted locally, no matter how fast the server itself is.

Think of it like ordering from a warehouse. If the warehouse is in Brisbane, your parcel arrives tomorrow. If it's in Texas, you're waiting a week. Same product, vastly different delivery time.

Most quality managed hosting providers offer Australian server locations. Many cheap shared hosting plans default to US servers. Check this before you sign up. Ask specifically where the server is located.

### So what should you actually spend?

Let's talk numbers, because I know that's what you really want to know.

Shared hosting typically runs between $5 and $25 a month. Managed hosting for a business website typically ranges from $39 to $150 a month, depending on the size and complexity of the site.

The difference is $15 to $125 a month. Call it $200 to $1,500 a year.

Now ask yourself: is your business website worth protecting for an extra $200 a year at minimum? Is consistent speed, proper security, Australian hosting, and real support worth a few dollars a day?

If your website is a hobby, shared hosting is fine. Genuinely. I'm not going to tell you to spend money you don't need to spend.

But if your website is a business tool — if it generates leads, processes sales, represents your brand, or is the first thing people see when they Google your company name — then managed hosting isn't an upgrade. It's the baseline.

### A quick note on "managed" vs actually managed

One more thing, because this catches people out. Not all "managed hosting" is created equal. Some hosts slap the word "managed" on a slightly-less-shared plan and call it a day.

Actual managed hosting should include: daily backups, proactive security updates, performance optimisation, uptime monitoring, staging environments for testing changes, and access to knowledgeable support. If your "managed" plan doesn't include those things, it's just expensive shared hosting with better branding.

Ask questions. Read the fine print. Or better yet, talk to someone who deals with hosting every day and can recommend the right setup for your specific situation.

## Article 5: Why Your Website Gets Traffic But No Enquiries (And How to Fix It)

This is one of the most frustrating situations a business owner can find themselves in, and I hear about it regularly.

"Richard, I'm getting traffic. Google Analytics shows hundreds, sometimes thousands of visitors a month. But nobody's calling. Nobody's filling out the form. Nobody's buying. What's going on?"

It's maddening, isn't it? You've done the hard work. You've invested in SEO, maybe some Google Ads, maybe social media. People are finding your site. They're clicking through. They're landing on your pages. And then... nothing. They leave. Like walking into a shop, looking around, and walking straight back out without saying a word.

The good news? This is almost always fixable. The bad news? The problems are usually hiding in plain sight, and you've probably looked at your own website so many times that you can't see them anymore.

Let me show you what to look for.

### Your messaging is unclear

This is the big one. The one that causes more lost enquiries than anything else.

A visitor lands on your homepage. They have about five seconds — maybe less — to understand three things: what you do, who you do it for, and why they should care. If your homepage headline is something like "Innovative Solutions for a Dynamic World," you've already lost them.

I'm not exaggerating. I've audited websites where I genuinely could not tell what the business did from the homepage. Beautiful design, lovely animations, zero clarity. It's like walking into a restaurant with no menu and no signage — the ambiance is great but you have no idea if they serve Italian or Thai.

Your headline should be plain, specific, and customer-focused. Not clever. Not creative. Clear. "We build custom kitchens for Brisbane homes" beats "Crafting Culinary Spaces for Modern Living" every single day of the week.

### Your calls to action are weak or missing

Here's an exercise. Open your website and try to find your primary call to action on the homepage. How long did it take? Could you find it without scrolling?

A call to action — "Get a Free Quote," "Book a Consultation," "Call Us Today" — needs to be obvious, specific, and repeated. Not buried in the footer. Not hidden behind a hamburger menu. Not a vague "Learn More" that could mean anything.

I reviewed a site recently where the only call to action was a phone number in the header. No contact form. No "get in touch" button anywhere on the page. Just a phone number in small text next to the logo. On mobile, you could barely see it. That business was leaving thousands of dollars on the table because they never explicitly asked visitors to take the next step.

People need to be told what to do next. That's not pushy — that's helpful. If someone's on your site, they're already interested. Make it ridiculously easy for them to act on that interest.

### You have no trust signals

Put yourself in your visitor's shoes. They've found your website through Google. They've never heard of you before. They have no idea if you're legitimate, competent, or trustworthy.

What on your website proves that you are?

Trust signals include: genuine customer testimonials with names and business names (not "J.S. from Sydney"), case studies showing real results, industry certifications and memberships, how long you've been in business, how many clients you've worked with, logos of businesses you've worked with, Google review ratings, before-and-after examples.

If your site has none of these, you're asking strangers to trust you based on nothing but your own claims. And in 2026, with scam websites everywhere and consumers more sceptical than ever, that's a big ask.

You don't need all of these. But you need some. A testimonial section on your homepage. A case study page. A "proudly serving Australian businesses since 2009" line. Something that says "we're real, we're good at this, and other people agree."

### Your mobile experience is fighting your visitors

Most of your traffic is probably coming from mobile devices. And on mobile, every friction point is amplified. A form with twelve fields? Nobody's filling that out on their phone. A phone number that isn't tap-to-call? Frustrating. Tiny buttons that are hard to hit with a thumb? Infuriating. A pop-up that covers the entire screen with no obvious way to close it? They're gone.

Check your analytics. Look at your conversion rate on desktop versus mobile. If desktop converts at 3% and mobile converts at 0.5%, your mobile experience is the problem. Not your traffic, not your offer, not your pricing. The experience.

### Your forms have too much friction

Speaking of forms. Let me ask you this: how many fields does your contact form have?

If the answer is more than five, you're losing people. Every additional field you add to a form reduces the completion rate. Name, email, phone, and message — that's all you need for an initial enquiry. You do not need their company name, their job title, their suburb, how they heard about you, their budget range, and their mother's maiden name just to say "I'm interested."

Collect the minimum you need to start a conversation. You can get the rest during that conversation. The form's job isn't to qualify the lead — it's to capture the lead.

And test your forms. Right now. Go fill one out yourself. Does the confirmation message work? Does the email actually arrive? Does it go to spam? You'd be amazed how many business owners have never tested their own contact form.

### Your content doesn't match visitor intent

Here's a subtler one. Your traffic might be high because you're ranking for keywords that bring the wrong audience.

If you're a commercial electrician ranking for "how to change a light switch," you're going to get a lot of DIY homeowners who will never, ever hire you. Traffic looks great. Enquiries don't exist. Because the people visiting your site aren't your people.

Check what keywords are actually driving your traffic. In Google Search Console, look at your top queries. Are they aligned with the services you sell? Or are they informational queries from people who just want a free answer?

This doesn't mean informational content is bad — it can build authority and trust. But if it's your only source of traffic, and your service pages aren't ranking for commercial terms, you've got a strategy problem.

### A quick self-audit you can do right now

Grab your phone. Open your website in a private browsing window so you see what a new visitor sees. Then answer these honestly:

- Within five seconds, can you tell what the business does and who it's for?
- Is there a clear call to action visible without scrolling?

## Article 6: Website Accessibility in Australia: What Small Businesses Need to Know About WCAG 2.2

Let me ask you something. If a customer in a wheelchair rolled up to your shop and the front door was too narrow, you'd fix it, right? You wouldn't even think twice.

Your website is no different. Except most business owners have no idea their digital front door is slamming shut on people every single day.

### The law most business owners don't know about

Here's the part that catches people off guard. The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 — the DDA — applies to websites. It has for years. The Australian Human Rights Commission has made it clear: if you offer goods or services to the public, your online presence is covered.

This isn't some hypothetical future regulation. It's current law. And complaints have been lodged. Real ones, against real Australian businesses — including small ones.

Now, I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. But I am someone who's built over 450 websites, and I can tell you that most of them — before I got involved — would have failed a basic accessibility check. Not because the owners were negligent. They just didn't know.

That's the gap I want to close today.

### So what does "accessible" actually mean?

When we talk about web accessibility, we're talking about making sure people with disabilities can use your website. That includes people who are blind or have low vision, people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people with motor impairments who can't use a mouse, and people with cognitive disabilities who need clear, simple layouts.

The international standard is called WCAG — Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. The current version is 2.2, and the level most businesses should aim for is AA. Not the bare minimum (A), and not the gold-plated version (AAA). AA is the sweet spot. It's what the Australian government references, and it's what most legal experts point to as the reasonable benchmark.

### What WCAG 2.2 AA actually asks for (in plain English)

I'm going to skip the technical spec talk and give you the plain version. WCAG 2.2 AA boils down to four principles. They even have a handy acronym: POUR.

**Perceivable** — Can people actually see or hear your content? This means images need alt text (a short description screen readers can read aloud), videos need captions, and your text needs enough contrast against its background so people with low vision can read it. You know those trendy light-grey-on-white designs? They look sleek. They're also basically invisible to anyone over 60.

**Operable** — Can people navigate your site without a mouse? A lot of people use keyboards only, or specialised input devices. Every button, link, and form field on your site should be reachable with the Tab key. Dropdown menus that only open on hover? Those are a problem.

**Understandable** — Does your site make sense? Are your forms labelled clearly? Do error messages actually explain what went wrong? Is your navigation consistent from page to page? This one's less about code and more about not confusing people.

**Robust** — Does your site work across different browsers and assistive technologies? This is where clean, well-structured code matters. A site built with proper HTML semantics — headings in the right order, landmarks like header and main and footer — works better for everyone, including screen readers.

WCAG 2.2 added a few new criteria on top of the older versions. Things like making sure clickable targets are at least 24 by 24 pixels (because not everyone has the fine motor control to hit a tiny link), and ensuring that help options like contact details are easy to find and consistently placed. Sensible stuff, honestly.

### The quick wins you can do this week

You don't need to rebuild your site from scratch. Here are straightforward fixes that make a genuine difference.

**Run a free audit.** Go to a tool called WAVE (wave.webaim.org) and plug in your URL. It'll flag the obvious issues — missing alt text, low contrast, empty links. It's not perfect, but it's a solid starting point.

**Add alt text to every image.** If the image conveys information, describe it. If it's purely decorative, mark it as such. "IMG_3847.jpg" as alt text helps nobody.

**Check your colour contrast.** There are free contrast checkers online. Your body text should have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 against its background. Headlines can get away with 3 to 1 because they're larger.

**Make sure your forms are labelled.** Every input field needs a visible label. Placeholder text that disappears when you start typing doesn't count — it's gone before you can reference it.

**Don't rely on colour alone.** If you mark required fields in red, also add an asterisk or the word "required." Someone who's colour-blind won't see the red.

**Check your heading structure.** Your page should have one H1, followed by H2s, then H3s. Don't skip levels. It's not just about visual hierarchy — screen readers use headings to navigate.

### When you need professional help

The quick wins get you a long way. But some things need a developer. Keyboard navigation issues, ARIA attributes, dynamic content that updates without alerting screen readers, accessible date pickers — these aren't DIY jobs.

When I build sites at RAVENCI, accessibility is baked into the process from day one. It's not an add-on. It's not a plugin you bolt on at the end and hope for the best. It's in the colour palette choices, the component design, the way forms are built, the heading structure — all of it.

Retrofitting accessibility onto an existing site is doable, but it's always more work than building it right the first time. That's true of most things in web development, honestly.

### This isn't just about legal risk

Yes, the legal angle is real, and yes, you should take it seriously. But here's the bigger picture: roughly one in five Australians lives with some form of disability. That's around four million people. They have money to spend, and they prefer to spend it with businesses that don't make them fight to use the website.

Accessible sites also tend to perform better in search. Google's algorithms favour well-structured, fast-loading, clearly labelled content — which is exactly what accessibility best practices produce. It's one of those rare cases where doing the right thing and doing the smart thing are the same thing.

A 1-second delay in page load time drops conversions by 7%. Let's make that real. Say your website generates $20,000 a month in leads or sales. A 1-second delay is costing you $1,400 a month. That's $16,800 a year.

### The speed killers I see every week

**Unoptimised images** — someone uploads a 4MB photo straight from their camera. Your hero image does not need to be 4000 pixels wide.

**Cheap shared hosting** — you're sharing a server with hundreds of other websites. It's like living in a share house where someone's always hogging the hot water.

**Too many plugins** — I've seen sites running 40-plus plugins. It's like carrying a toolbox, a lawnmower, and a kayak into a meeting.

**No caching** — your server rebuilds the entire page from scratch every time someone visits. Like cooking a new meal for every customer instead of having a few dishes ready to serve.

**Third-party scripts gone wild** — analytics, chat widgets, Facebook pixels, popups. Each one is an external request your site has to make before it can finish loading.

## Article 14: What to Expect From an eCommerce Website Build in 2026

If you're a Brisbane retailer thinking about selling online — or rebuilding an existing online store — this one's for you. I want to set realistic expectations about what an eCommerce build actually involves, because there's a lot of misinformation out there.

### Let's talk money first

A legitimate eCommerce website in 2026 will cost between $8,000 and $30,000 or more.

**$8,000 - $12,000** — A clean store on Shopify or BigCommerce with a customised theme, up to about 50 products, payment gateway, shipping for Australia, and basic SEO.

**$12,000 - $20,000** — More products (100-500), custom functionality, POS or inventory integration, advanced filtering, bespoke design.

**$20,000 - $30,000+** — Thousands of SKUs, wholesale/retail pricing, custom checkout, ERP integration, or fully custom headless commerce.

Anyone quoting $2,000 is either cutting corners or will hit you with change requests until you've spent $10,000 anyway.

### Choosing your platform

**Shopify** — Most popular for good reason. Handles hosting, security, updates. Best for straightforward catalogues under $2M online revenue.

**BigCommerce** — No transaction fees on any plan. Better for larger catalogues and B2B.

**WooCommerce** — Flexible, no transaction fees beyond your gateway's cut. Requires more maintenance. Best if you already have a strong WordPress site.

**Custom/Headless** — For complex needs. Fast, flexible, future-proof. Most expensive, requires ongoing developer support.

### Australian payment options

At minimum: credit/debit cards via Stripe (1.75% + 30c), Afterpay/Zip Pay (it's an expectation, not a trend), Apple Pay and Google Pay, and PayPal.

### The realistic timeline

4 to 8 weeks from kickoff to launch. Weeks 1-2: discovery and planning. Weeks 2-4: design and development. Weeks 4-6: product loading and content. Weeks 6-8: testing and launch.

The biggest delay is always waiting for product photos and descriptions from the client.

### What to have ready

Product information (name, description, price, weight, photos), brand assets, shipping information, business policies (returns, privacy, terms), tax setup, and goals/KPIs.

### Don't build it and forget it

The single biggest mistake I see is treating the launch as the finish line. It's the starting line. After launch, you need to watch analytics, optimise product pages, run A/B tests on checkout, build your email list, and continuously improve.

## Article 15: Your Website's Brand Problem: Why a New Logo Isn't the Same as a Brand Identity

I get this call about once a month.

"Richard, I need a new logo. Our brand feels outdated."

And I always ask the same follow-up question: "When you say brand, what exactly do you mean?"

Nine times out of ten, the answer is the logo. And look, I get it. The logo is the most visible part of your brand. But it's not the brand. Not even close.

A logo is to a brand what a front door is to a house. It's the first thing people see, sure. But if the rooms behind it are a mess — mismatched furniture, peeling wallpaper, a kitchen from 1987 — that nice front door isn't fooling anyone for long.

### What a brand actually is

Your brand is the total experience someone has with your business. Every touchpoint. The words on your website. The tone of your emails. The way your receptionist answers the phone. The look of your invoices. Your social media posts. The packaging your product arrives in.

### The brand consistency problem

Here's a scenario I see constantly. A Brisbane business has a logo from 2016, a website from 2019 with a slightly different colour palette, social media graphics from Canva templates, business cards in one shade of green, a shop fit-out in a different shade of green, and email signatures with the old logo.

Every touchpoint is sending a different signal. Research from Lucidpress found that consistent brand presentation across all platforms increases revenue by up to 23%.

### The components of a real brand identity

**Brand Strategy** — Who is your ideal customer? What makes you different? What's your brand personality?

**Visual Identity** — Primary logo, logo variations, colour palette (exact HEX and CMYK codes), typography system, photography style, graphic elements, and usage guidelines.

**Brand Voice and Messaging** — How you sound in writing. Value proposition, key messages, elevator pitch. At RAVENCI, our core message is "Built properly, still working in five years." That informs everything we write.

**Customer Experience Touchpoints** — How you answer the phone, what your proposals look like, your onboarding experience, your follow-up process.

### The logo-only trap

When you invest in a logo alone, nothing else changes. Your colour palette doesn't quite match. Your social media uses random fonts. Your team writes copy in whatever tone feels right. Six months later, the new logo feels just as disjointed as the old one.

I had a client — a Brisbane professional services firm — who'd been through three logos in five years. When we finally did a proper brand identity project and rebuilt their website to embody all of it, their close rate on proposals went up within the first quarter. Not because the logo was better, but because every touchpoint finally told the same story.

Your brand is not your logo. Your logo is just the face. The brand is the entire personality, reputation, and experience behind it. Get that right, and the logo almost designs itself.
